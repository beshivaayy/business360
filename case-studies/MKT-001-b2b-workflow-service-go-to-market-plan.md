# MKT-001 — B2B Workflow Service Go-to-Market Plan

> **Status:** Portfolio case study — synthetic strategy exercise  
> **Pillar:** Marketing & Business Growth  
> **Data:** Illustrative assumptions and fictional company context; no real client or campaign results are claimed.

## 1. Executive brief

This case study designs a focused go-to-market (GTM) plan for a fictional B2B services firm offering workflow support to mid-sized businesses. The commercial challenge is not simply generating leads: it is identifying a narrow buyer segment, proving operational value, building trust, and converting qualified demand into profitable, repeatable engagements.

**Recommendation:** Start with one industry segment and one costly workflow problem. Use founder-led discovery, a tightly scoped paid pilot, evidence-based content, and account-based outreach. Expand only after the firm validates willingness to pay, delivery capacity, and repeatability.

## 2. Fictional business context

The hypothetical company provides managed workflow operations and quality-support services to mid-market businesses. It has limited brand awareness, a small sales team, and no reliable historical funnel data.

### Planning assumptions — not market facts
- Initial focus: one narrowly defined B2B segment.
- Sales motion: consultative, multi-stakeholder.
- Offer: paid diagnostic or pilot followed by a recurring service.
- Growth constraint: limited sales capacity and need to protect delivery quality.
- Budget: to be set only after channel tests and unit economics are known.

## 3. Market and customer discovery

Do not select an industry based on intuition alone. Score candidate segments against:
1. Frequency and cost of the workflow problem.
2. Urgency and executive visibility.
3. Access to decision-makers.
4. Ability to prove value within a short pilot.
5. Procurement complexity and sales-cycle length.
6. Fit with current delivery capability.
7. Data sensitivity and compliance burden.

### Interview plan

Conduct 12–15 structured discovery interviews across buyers, operational users, and influencers. This is a proposed research sample, not completed research.

Ask:
- How is the workflow handled today?
- Where do delays, errors, or handoffs occur?
- What happens commercially when the process fails?
- What have you already tried?
- Who owns the budget and approval?
- What evidence would justify a pilot?
- What would prevent adoption?

Record verbatim problem statements, current workaround, cost proxy, buying trigger, decision process, and disconfirming evidence. Avoid leading questions about the proposed solution.

## 4. Ideal customer profile and buying committee

### Initial ICP hypothesis
A mid-market organisation with recurring, measurable, people-intensive workflows; visible quality or turnaround pressure; a named process owner; and sufficient volume to justify an external service.

### Buying committee
| Role | Likely concern | Message |
|---|---|---|
| Economic buyer | Cost, risk, business outcome | Measurable value and controlled commitment |
| Operations leader | Reliability, capacity, visibility | Clear workflow ownership and service controls |
| Quality leader | Error rates and acceptance | Transparent QA, escalation, and audit trail |
| Procurement/legal | Vendor risk and terms | Defined scope, security, and accountability |
| Frontline user | Ease of adoption | Practical onboarding and low-friction handoffs |

This is a hypothesis to validate by segment.

## 5. Positioning and messaging

**Positioning statement:** For operations teams facing recurring workflow bottlenecks, the service provides structured execution, measurable quality controls, and delivery visibility—without requiring the buyer to redesign the entire operating model at once.

### Message hierarchy
- **Problem:** Work gets delayed or returned because handoffs, instructions, and ownership are inconsistent.
- **Outcome:** More predictable workflow execution with transparent quality and delivery measures.
- **Proof required:** Baseline, pilot results, customer references where permissioned, and documented operating controls.
- **Risk reversal:** A scoped pilot with agreed success criteria and an exit decision.

Avoid unsupported claims such as “industry-leading,” “zero errors,” or guaranteed savings.

## 6. Offer architecture

| Offer | Purpose | Commercial logic | Exit criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discovery diagnostic | Understand current process and quantify problem | Fixed scope/fee or credited diagnostic | Problem and buyer ownership validated |
| Paid pilot | Test delivery model on limited scope | Fixed pilot fee with explicit assumptions | Success criteria met and buyer confirms value |
| Managed service | Repeatable ongoing delivery | Recurring contract tied to scope and service levels | Margin, quality, and retention remain healthy |
| Expansion | Add workflow or volume | Change request and capacity review | Delivery capability confirmed |

Price should be derived from delivery cost, service complexity, risk, buyer value, and competitive alternatives—not guessed for the portfolio.

## 7. Channel strategy

Prioritise channels by buyer access and learning speed, not vanity reach.

| Channel | Role in funnel | Test | Primary measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founder-led account outreach | Reach named ICP accounts | Personalised problem-led outreach | Qualified conversations per target account |
| Referral/partner network | Borrow trust | Partner introduction experiment | Qualified opportunities and conversion |
| LinkedIn thought leadership | Build credibility | Practical workflow teardown posts | Relevant buyer engagement and inbound conversations |
| Webinars/roundtables | Educate buying committee | Small problem-specific session | Attendee-to-discovery conversion |
| Search/content | Capture active demand | One high-intent problem page | Qualified organic enquiries |

Do not scale paid acquisition until conversion economics and ICP are clearer.

## 8. Funnel and qualification

Proposed funnel:
**Target accounts → Engaged accounts → Discovery → Qualified opportunity → Paid pilot → Contract → Renewal/expansion**

Define a qualified opportunity only when the problem, impact, owner, decision path, timing, and plausible budget are understood.

Track stage conversion by cohort and source. Do not treat raw lead volume as commercial success.

## 9. 90-day GTM execution plan

| Period | Actions | Deliverables | Decision gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Days 1–30 | Interview buyers; score segments; map alternatives; test problem language | ICP v1, interview synthesis, positioning hypotheses | Select one segment only if evidence supports urgency and access |
| Days 31–60 | Build diagnostic/pilot offer; create proof assets; run targeted outreach | Offer sheet, pilot scope, outreach sequence, discovery script | Continue if qualified buyers engage and pilot terms are viable |
| Days 61–90 | Deliver controlled pilot(s); document outcomes; test renewal intent | Pilot report, case evidence, commercial model | Scale only if customer value and delivery economics are credible |

## 10. KPI framework

Use the blank [GTM funnel tracker](../templates/gtm-funnel-tracker.csv) to capture period- and segment-level funnel data. It contains no sample performance claims.

| KPI | Definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| ICP account coverage | Target accounts with verified contact and fit ÷ target accounts | Measures market access |
| Positive engagement rate | Accounts with meaningful response ÷ contacted accounts | Tests message relevance |
| Discovery-to-qualified rate | Qualified opportunities ÷ completed discovery meetings | Tests qualification |
| Pilot conversion rate | Paid pilots ÷ qualified opportunities | Tests offer strength |
| Pilot-to-contract rate | Ongoing contracts ÷ completed pilots | Tests delivered value |
| Sales cycle | Median days from qualified opportunity to decision | Informs cash and capacity planning |
| CAC | Attributable acquisition cost ÷ new customers | Tests acquisition efficiency |
| Gross margin | Revenue less direct delivery costs ÷ revenue | Protects commercial viability |
| Retention/expansion | Renewed or expanded eligible accounts ÷ eligible accounts | Tests durable value |

Set numerical targets only after the first baseline cohort. Segment results by source, customer type, and offer.

## 11. Risks and controls

- **ICP too broad:** restrict initial segment and revisit using interview evidence.
- **Overpromising:** use scoped outcomes and documented assumptions.
- **Pilot does not convert:** agree decision criteria and buying process before starting.
- **Sales outruns delivery:** require capacity and quality sign-off before contract.
- **Long procurement cycle:** map stakeholders early and maintain a realistic pipeline.
- **Weak attribution:** use consistent CRM stages and source definitions.
- **Sensitive information:** collect only necessary data and use approved security terms.

## 12. Decision framework

Continue the GTM motion when discovery confirms a costly problem, qualified buyers can be reached, pilots are paid or otherwise commercially meaningful, and delivery can meet agreed standards.

Adjust when the problem is real but the segment, offer, message, or channel is wrong.

Stop or reposition when repeated discovery fails to validate urgency, budget ownership, or willingness to change.

## 13. Evidence and limitations

- All company details and commercial scenarios are fictional.
- No market-size, competitor-share, pricing, conversion, CAC, or revenue claim is asserted.
- The 12–15 interview plan is proposed research, not completed fieldwork.
- Channel performance and buyer roles vary by segment and must be validated.
- This is a decision framework, not a guarantee of commercial results.

ICP design, customer discovery, positioning, offer architecture, channel selection, funnel design, KPI governance, 90-day execution planning, and commercial risk management.
