# STR-001 — Market-Entry Decision for a B2B Services Company

> **Status:** Portfolio case study — synthetic strategy exercise  
> **Pillar:** Management & Business Strategy  
> **Data:** Fictional scenario and illustrative scoring; not a real market study or investment recommendation.

## 1. Executive brief

A fictional B2B services company wants to enter a new industry vertical. Leadership has limited sales capacity and must choose where to focus without spreading delivery resources too thin.

**Strategic question:** Which segment should the company enter first, and what evidence is required before committing meaningful resources?

**Recommendation:** Use a staged market-entry decision. Screen segments using customer pain, addressability, delivery fit, competitive alternatives, sales-cycle friction, and risk. Select one beachhead only after primary research validates the scoring assumptions.

## 2. Situation and constraints

The hypothetical firm has an existing service capability but limited vertical-specific proof. It cannot afford a broad launch, large fixed-cost buildout, or multiple simultaneous sales motions.

Constraints:
- Limited management and business-development bandwidth.
- Need to protect current customer delivery.
- Unknown segment-level willingness to pay.
- Unvalidated competitor and substitute landscape.
- Potential data, procurement, and regulatory requirements.

## 3. Strategic options

| Option | Description | Advantage | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Broad launch | Target several industries at once | Larger apparent opportunity | Diffuse messaging and execution |
| B. Single beachhead | Focus one segment and use pilots | Faster learning and clearer positioning | Segment may be too narrow |
| C. Channel-led entry | Enter through a partner | Access and trust | Partner dependency and lower control |
| D. Defer entry | Strengthen current market first | Lower immediate risk | Opportunity cost and slower learning |

## 4. Segment-screening framework

Score each candidate segment from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong). Use evidence-backed scoring; the example below is deliberately blank so the team does not mistake invented scores for research.

| Criterion | Suggested weight | Evidence needed |
|---|---:|---|
| Severity/frequency of problem | 20% | Buyer interviews, process data |
| Addressable demand | 15% | Verified account universe and spend proxies |
| Delivery capability fit | 20% | Skills, capacity, process requirements |
| Access to decision-makers | 10% | Reachability and buying committee |
| Competitive differentiation | 10% | Alternatives and buyer switching criteria |
| Sales-cycle/procurement friction | 10% | Buyer process and legal requirements |
| Margin and repeatability potential | 10% | Cost-to-serve and willingness to pay |
| Data/security/regulatory fit | 5% | Risk assessment and control requirements |

Weighted score = sum of (criterion score × weight). Weights are initial management choices, not objective facts. Run sensitivity analysis: does the preferred segment change when the top weights move by a reasonable range?

## 5. Research plan

Use the blank [strategy options matrix](../templates/strategy-options-matrix.csv) to record alternatives, criteria, evidence references, assumptions, owners, and review dates. Scores are structured judgement and should be sensitivity-tested.

### Secondary research
- Define the category and adjacent substitutes.
- Identify potential customer types and buying triggers.
- Review public industry reports, company filings, procurement notices, and competitor service descriptions.
- Separate market size estimates from the realistically serviceable segment.
- Record source date, methodology, geography, and limitations.

### Primary research
Conduct structured interviews with prospective buyers, operational users, and relevant partners. Validate current workflow, pain severity, existing alternatives, buying authority, procurement steps, and willingness to run a pilot.

**Evidence hierarchy:** audited/public primary data and direct buyer evidence; reputable industry research; credible secondary reporting; directional commentary. Label estimates and assumptions.

## 6. Strategic fit and capability gap

Assess:
- Service capability and domain knowledge.
- Talent availability and training lead time.
- Delivery capacity and quality assurance.
- Technology and data handling requirements.
- Sales access and referenceability.
- Working-capital and contract requirements.

A segment with attractive demand is not attractive if the company cannot serve it reliably or profitably.

## 7. Entry thesis and beachhead design

A viable entry thesis should state:
1. The specific customer segment.
2. The operational problem and why it matters now.
3. The service outcome the company can credibly deliver.
4. Why the current alternatives are insufficient.
5. The proof required to win the first few customers.
6. The economics and delivery conditions required to scale.

The beachhead should be narrow enough to build repeatable proof, but large enough to support a sustainable business.

## 8. Staged execution roadmap

| Stage | Actions | Output | Gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Screen | Longlist segments; apply weighted criteria | Segment shortlist | Reject poor capability/risk fit |
| 1. Validate | Buyer interviews and competitor/substitute review | Evidence-backed entry thesis | Confirm pain, buyer, urgency |
| 2. Prove | Run a scoped pilot with clear acceptance criteria | Pilot evidence and unit economics | Confirm value and delivery feasibility |
| 3. Establish | Build repeatable sales and delivery playbook | Referenceable proof and operating model | Confirm repeatability |
| 4. Expand | Add adjacent accounts or segment only | Expansion plan | Capacity, margin, and quality guardrails met |

## 9. Investment and economics

Build a segment-level model using:
- Revenue per engagement and expected frequency.
- Direct labour, QA, tooling, onboarding, and support costs.
- Sales and procurement cost.
- Working-capital and payment terms.
- Utilisation and capacity constraints.
- Retention and expansion assumptions.

Use conservative/base/upside scenarios. Do not use a single-point forecast as certainty. Show break-even volume and sensitivity to utilisation, price, rework, and sales-cycle length.

## 10. Management dashboard

| Dimension | KPI | Management question |
|---|---|---|
| Market validation | Qualified buyer interviews; validated pain themes | Is the problem real and urgent? |
| Commercial traction | Qualified opportunities; paid pilot conversion | Will buyers commit? |
| Delivery | Acceptance, cycle time, on-time delivery | Can we deliver reliably? |
| Economics | Gross margin; cost-to-serve; cash conversion | Is the model viable? |
| Strategic learning | Assumptions validated/invalidated | What did we learn? |
| Risk | Open security, legal, and dependency issues | What could block entry? |

## 11. Governance and risk

- Assign an executive sponsor and accountable entry lead.
- Use explicit stage gates; do not let sunk cost dictate continuation.
- Protect existing customer service levels during the pilot.
- Review data protection, security, contracting, and sector-specific obligations before handling customer data.
- Record assumptions, decisions, dissent, and evidence in a decision log.
- Establish stop criteria before investment.

## 12. Decision memo template

**Decision requested:** Enter / defer / reject / run a limited validation.

**Chosen segment:** To be completed after research.

**Evidence supporting the decision:** Buyer evidence, demand evidence, capability fit, economics.

**Key assumptions:** Explicitly listed with owner and validation date.

**Downside case:** Conditions under which the entry fails.

**Investment requested:** People, time, tools, and working capital.

**Stage-gate date:** Set by leadership.

**Accountable owner:** Named role.

## 13. Limitations and evidence register

- No real geography, industry, market size, competitor, or customer has been selected.
- Weighted criteria and weights are a proposed framework.
- No actual market research or interviews are claimed.
- Commercial outcomes require verified cost and buyer data.
- This case study demonstrates strategic decision architecture, not a completed market-entry project.

## 14. Portfolio capabilities demonstrated

Strategic option analysis, market screening, evidence hierarchy, capability assessment, scenario planning, stage-gate governance, unit-economics thinking, and executive decision communication.
